Can I trust evidence I did not personally gather?

A twenty-one-day expedition crosses the High Pass and basin toward Creston and Hollis. The field work is only half the challenge. The other half is learning to trust evidence gathered, preserved, and remembered by other people.

Inside the story

HIGH PASS • NORTH BASIN

TWENTY-ONE DAYS. ONE HIGH PASS.

THE ROAD VANISHES INTO THE BASIN.

THE AGREEMENT CHANGES ON THE FAR SIDE.

Seventeen-year-old Sam Calder begins his longest journey: three weeks over the High Pass with a mixed survey crew—and without Mara, whose apprenticeship starts the day after he leaves. Beyond the last gravel, the freight road dissolves into a basin where the best-looking track is often the wrong one.

On the far side, northern archives preserve a five-year review clause missing from southern copies of the Compact. Sam wants to announce that the old agreement expired. A local historian asks the harder question: what does an expired document change after sixty years of new roads, fallen bridges, and lives built around the closure?

Then a photograph filed under the wrong heading reveals Zero House. It is not a house. It is the enormous transfer station where roads once came together.

A missing clause can change the history. It cannot repair the road.

Sam’s progression

Sam moves from personally checking everything to building a reliable chain of provenance, delegation, and review.
